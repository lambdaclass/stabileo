Auxetic spring networks

After Doron Grossman, Arezki Boudaoud,“Predicting the mechanical properties of spring networks”, Physical Review Research (2026), doi:10.1103/17sg-gzhl.

Pull a strip of Hookean springs and read how much it narrows. Shake the nodes of the triangular lattice and it starts to widen instead: it becomes auxetic. The network on the right is solved in your browser as a truss on Stabileo’s engine, and the same strip opens in the real Stabileo editor. No reference code is public (“available at request”), so we compare with the paper’s published numbers, closed forms and figures, and with exact results.

What the paper shows

Many materials, from metamaterial lattices to cell membranes, are modelled as networks of Hookean springs. Grossman and Boudaoud derive the elastic continuum that such a network behaves like, using only its geometry and topology, and they compute the nonaffine displacements: the local deviations from a uniform stretch that make disordered networks softer than the uniform-strain estimate. They check the theory against simulations of a stretched strip for three families: ordered triangular lattices, a honeycomb that turns re-entrant, and a “foam” made by randomly displacing the nodes of a triangular lattice, which becomes auxetic (negative Poisson’s ratio: it widens when pulled) as the disorder grows.

The model we solve

Springs e join nodes; each has stiffness ke, rest length ℓ̄e and current length ℓe, and the energy is E = ½ Σe ke (ℓe − ℓ̄e)² (Eq. 3). The viewer shows axial force on the deformed truss. We compute the following responses.

  • Linear strip test. A 13 × 52 strip with 824 nodes, compared with the paper’s approximately 676 vertices (Sec. V A, pp. 9–10). We hold complete end rows selected before disorder and prescribe a vertical increment ε × 52; the paper sets the top vertices to y = 52(1 + ε). These are different boundary prescriptions. Width is measured between the nodes nearest mid-height. The production truss solver agrees below 10⁻¹³ (recorded; tolerance 10⁻⁸).
  • Exact effective moduli. A periodic cell under an imposed macroscopic strain, with every node free to relax: the stiffness that remains is the network’s elastic tensor C, including the nonaffine softening, with no boundary effects. The uniform-strain (affine) tensor is the same sum without relaxation.
  • Prestress (an addition, not in the paper’s computations): rest lengths shortened by a fraction, the network relaxed to equilibrium by Newton’s method, and the linear response taken about that state, tension stiffening included.

Comparison with the paper

Foam: the auxetic transition

The periodic directional ν along y uses 10 seeds of 26 × 26 cells, crosses zero at η = 0.447, and reaches -0.146 at η = 0.5 (-0.115 at 0.49). The paper reports approximately 0.46 and −0.1. This cross-protocol comparison falls within all ten upward Fig. 5 error bars plus 0.003 digitisation; it does not validate the strip. Fig. 5 reports ten realizations; our seeds are our own choice.

The direct strip comparison lies outside the Fig. 5 error bars plus 0.003 at η = 0; 0.05; 0.1; 0.2. The perfect strip reads 0.371; widths 26 and 52 give 0.354 and 0.345, extrapolating to 0.336. This is a comparison of our finite linear strip with the published simulation markers, not a reproduction of their geometry or boundary prescription.

Honeycomb: Eq. (23)

A honeycomb of unit edges whose opposing corners are 2ℓ apart is regular at ℓ = 1 and re-entrant below ℓ = ½. The paper triangulates each hexagon with a centre node and springs 10⁻³ as stiff as the edges, and compares with the closed form ν(ℓ) = −(2 − 4ℓ)(ℓ + ½)/(3 + 4ℓ − 4ℓ²). Our periodic network agrees to 3.4 × 10⁻³ with those springs and to 3.4 × 10⁻⁶ with springs 10⁻⁶ as stiff: the difference is the soft springs’ own stiffness. ·

Ordered lattices: an observation about spring constants

For ordered triangular lattices the paper gives a closed form, Supplemental Eq. (9), for ν as a function of the cell shape (φ, ψ) and orientation θ, plotted in its Fig. 4. A Bravais lattice has no nonaffine displacements, so the exact answer is the uniform-strain one, which we compute independently. Supplemental Eq. (9) and the calculation line of Fig. 7 (Young’s modulus of the (3, 2) lattice, read from the PDF’s vector data, times the area of one triangle) coincide with that affine result for spring constants ke ∝ 1/ℓ̄e², below 10⁻¹⁴ and within 6.1 × 10⁻⁴ respectively. With equal spring constants, ν = 1/6 at (φ, ψ, θ) = (1, 0.5, 0), while Eq. (9) gives 8/27. The comparison therefore uses the inverse-square spring convention. ·

Select an ordered lattice (without dilution or prestress) to plot Eq. (9) against both spring laws.

We could not reproduce the simulation dots of Fig. 7: the paper does not say how its strip simulation measures Young’s modulus, and our reading (vertical force through mid-height over width and strain) does not reproduce those dots.

Rigidity and connectivity (an independent check)

Independent check: five samples at nominal z = 3 have G/k < 10⁻⁶; five at z = 5.4 have G/k ≥ 0.1. Maxwell counting gives z = 4 (see Jacobs and Thorpe, PRL 75, 4051, 1995). ·

Limitations

  • No reference code is public. Comparisons rest on the paper’s text, its closed forms, the vector data of Figs. 7–9 and a digitisation of the raster Fig. 5 (±0.002).
  • We have not implemented the paper’s own semi-analytical method (the nonaffine tensors W of its Eq. 20 in the mean-field approximation); we compare with its results.
  • Linear response about equilibrium. The paper instead minimizes nonlinear energy at 1% strain.
  • Autoplay uses recorded native solver frames. Live recomputation runs in a terminable worker.
  • The strip’s free edges bias the measured ν by O(1/width); the periodic moduli have no such bias.
  • At η = 0.5, two standard errors of our directional periodic mean are ±0.0109; the plotted band is one sample standard deviation.
  • Agreement with a paper is not validation against experiments, and nothing here is an engineering design result.
